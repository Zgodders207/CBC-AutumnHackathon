Roadmap and assumptions

Short steps that fit £300 and 5 to 10 hours a week. Each tests the idea before real money goes in. The steps run one after another, not at the same time.

In total: 16 weeks and about £140 of your £300, which leaves £160 spare.

  1. Test demand

    Weeks 1 to 2Cost £20

    Post one simple offer in two local groups and count real replies.

  2. First paying customer

    Weeks 3 to 6Cost £120

    Fixed-price tune-ups in one postcode, so travel stays short.

  3. Price it fairly

    Weeks 7 to 8Cost £0

    No new spend: compare your time per job with what customers paid, then set your price.

  4. Grow or stop

    Weeks 9 to 16Cost £0

    If 10 paid jobs haven’t come in by the end of week 16, stop before buying more kit.

What this plan assumes

  • You own most of your tools, so the £300 only has to cover extras.
  • You can cycle to jobs, so travel costs next to nothing.
  • You can keep two evenings a week free for bookings.
  • You work alone, with no premises and no staff.

If any of these isn’t true, the times and costs above will change. Edit your answers and run the check again.

Improved business plan

Who it’s for

Commuters and families on one Gateshead estate who can’t easily get to a bike shop during opening hours.

What you sell

A home visit for common repairs, priced as a call-out fee plus parts, booked on two set evenings a week, with a winter service plan for regulars.

The market

Bike shops already serve Gateshead, but most keep shop hours and expect you to bring the bike in. Your edge is convenience, not price, so test whether people will pay a little more for a visit at home. Check local prices and rules yourself before you fix yours.

How you’ll find customers

Free safety checks at a community event, then word of mouth on one estate before spreading to the next.

The money

Your £300 covers tools and extras. The roadmap spends about £140 on testing, so there is room for a small buffer. Each job earns a call-out fee plus parts, and your travel time counts as a cost. Set your final prices only after you’ve seen what customers actually pay in the first weeks.

Risks and what you’ll do about them

Quiet winters: sell a service plan before autumn. Travel eating your hours: stay on one estate until jobs are steady. Weak early results: stop at week 16 if 10 paid jobs haven’t come in, rather than buying more kit.

Where to get proper advice

This example doesn’t list any local support, because we only list support we’ve checked. Until then, speak to an accountant about tax before your first paid job, check what insurance you need before you take payment, and check your council’s rules on trading from home or in public places.
